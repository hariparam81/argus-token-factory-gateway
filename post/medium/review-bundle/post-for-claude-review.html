A Framework for Running a Shared Inference Service

Five tenets decide whether tenants can trust it. Here's the set, with the reasoning behind each.

Harish Parameswaran

The first piece in this series measured what a finished task costs across open models. It picked a model per use case from the measurements. This piece goes to the other side of the counter. It asks what it takes to sell that inference to other people's workloads. [5]

A token factory is simple to describe. Customers send prompts. Shared GPUs run them. Customers get completions and a bill. The model endpoint is the easy part of that business. It is also the part everyone demos.

The hard part is everything around the endpoint. Tenants share everything that makes the service cheap: the GPUs, the model, the prefix cache. Sharing creates the risks a provider has to answer for. A tenant's prompt can leave traces in a shared cache. An agent can spend money at machine speed, sometimes with no clear owner attached. An enterprise buyer will ask about all three in the first security review, and the answers decide whether the factory gets the workload.

That layer has a name in this framework: the factory gateway. It is everything a call passes through before the model sees it: how it proves who it is, what it may spend, whose data it may touch, and what record it leaves.

This post proposes five tenets for that layer. Each one is stated as a principle, with the practice that keeps it and the evidence behind it. The last section describes a small instance I built to test whether the five can hold at once. It ran a real workload. Its failures are reported with its results.

The five tenets

Five tenets describe the terms on which the factory sells. Each gets a section below. This is the whole argument on one screen.

  1. Lowest cost. Inference is the production workload now. Price decides who wins it. A factory that cannot meter its own cost cannot price a token.
  2. Federate with the customer's identity provider. Enterprises already run identity. A factory that issues its own passwords becomes one more credential to leak and one more lifecycle to manage.
  3. Isolate each tenant's data and cache. One tenant's prompts must leave no trace another tenant can measure. Without this, the shared cache is a leak with an API.
  4. Meter and manage spend. Every call is metered to the tenant, the user and the agent behind it. Budgets cap each level. The tenant can see where the money went.
  5. Audit every call. Every request leaves a row naming who made it and on whose behalf. Without the row, the other four tenets are claims.

How a call moves through a factory that keeps these tenets:

1Lowest cost

Choose the model and the hardware by cost per outcome. Price the token from your own meter.

Inference is now the majority of AI compute in production. Menlo's 2025 market data puts inference at 74% of workload among startups and 49% among enterprises, with enterprise LLM spend more than doubling in a year [1]. The same open model is served by more than twenty providers at prices that differ by multiples, and comparable open models run about 90% below closed ones [3]. Cost management has become a formal discipline because the bills kept surprising people. 98% of FinOps practitioners now manage AI spend [2]. McKinsey's work with CIOs starts from the same pressure. Token demand is growing faster than budgets [4].

Two habits follow. First, pick models by measurement. The workbench behind the first piece in this series ran the same tasks across eight open models and chose per use case on accuracy per dollar [5]. The winner for document questions and for Text2SQL was a 7B coder model, at a fraction of the larger models' cost. Second, derive the price you charge from the hardware you run. Tokens per hour on a known card, at a known rate. A price built that way survives a customer's own arithmetic.

In the run described at the end of this post, the instance priced its token exactly this way. The pod's hourly rate divided by its measured throughput gave about $0.198 per million tokens. Every call was metered at that rate.

Cost is the tenet the other four are not allowed to break. A control that doubles the price of a token is a control the market will route around.

2Federate with the customer's identity provider

Customers sign in with the identity they already run. The factory's part is verification.

Attackers mostly log in. Valid-account abuse sits behind 35% of cloud incidents in CrowdStrike's 2026 threat report [6]. Microsoft's 2026 defence report records password attacks falling while token theft and adversary-in-the-middle phishing more than doubled their share [7]. Verizon's 2026 breach report counts more than 22,000 breaches, with credential abuse still present in 13% [8]. A factory that hands out its own passwords joins that problem. Federation avoids it. The factory inherits the customer's sign-in controls, its lifecycle and its revocation. It walks into the security review with the customer's own controls as the answer.

The factory accepts OIDC tokens from the tenant's provider and checks them properly: signature against the issuer's keys, asymmetric algorithms only, exact issuer, the gateway as audience, expiry required. Federation and assertion handling are settled standards work [9]. The tenant is read from the gateway's own registry, keyed by issuer and subject. A tenant named in a client header counts for nothing. Agents get their own path. Token exchange under RFC 8693 names the user as subject and the agent in an actor claim, so a delegated call carries both identities [10]. Security events after login can flow back to the tenant through the Shared Signals standards [11].

In the run, Tenant B never held a factory credential. Its users and its agent called with tokens from its own provider. The same budgets and limits applied. Eleven broken or forged credentials were sent at the gateway in testing. All eleven were refused.

Federation is the tenet that makes the factory adoptable. Procurement can approve what its own identity team already controls.

3Isolate each tenant's data and cache

One tenant's prompts leave no trace another tenant can measure.

Shared inference is cheap partly because of the prefix cache. When many calls share a long opening (a system prompt, a document, a set of examples), the endpoint computes it once and reuses it. On document work the shared opening is most of the prompt, so the cache is most of the margin.

The cache is also shared. The cache is keyed by the text itself. A hit is visible. The call answers faster and reports cached tokens. That gives any tenant a measuring instrument. Guess a piece of another tenant's prompt and send it. Watch the timing. A fast answer means the guess already sits in the cache. Repeat the guessing and the prompt can be rebuilt piece by piece. Published attacks do exactly this with ordinary API access, and report reconstruction from 89% up to 100% against servers that do not separate tenants [12] [13]. The channel is tracked as a vulnerability in vLLM [14].

What leaks is whatever tenants put in prompts: system prompts, source code, and the documents loaded for question answering, including the contracts and records inside them. The victim sees nothing. The probing calls look like normal traffic. Isolation has to be its own mechanism, separate from authentication [15].

Salting closes this channel. The gateway mixes a per-tenant value into the cache key and discards any value the client sends. The same text from two tenants now lands in different cache entries. A tenant still hits its own cache, so the economics survive. What salting does not do is isolate the machine itself. Tenants still share a scheduler and a GPU. vLLM's own documentation says the salt is not an isolation boundary [14]. Full isolation is a dedicated instance per tenant. Those are different products at different prices.

The run measured both directions. With salting on, Tenant B's first call on a paper Tenant A had warmed read 0 of 4,481 cached tokens. With salting off, the same call read 4,480 of 4,481.

Isolation is the tenet that makes sharing survivable. Tenants accept shared hardware when co-tenancy cannot read them.

4Meter and manage spend

Meter every call to the principal who made it: the tenant, the user, and the agent where there is one. Cap each level. Show the tenant the breakdown.

Consumption pricing fails open. Mandiant and Google Cloud document an agent loop that ran up about $50,000 in under an hour across more than 15,000 calls [16]. McKinsey's 2026 survey found 93% of organisations exceeding their AI budgets [17]. A FinOps survey found most teams hold cost policies without live visibility into the spend those policies govern [18]. A monthly invoice is where these stories end. The factory's job is to end them earlier, at a number someone can still act on.

Metering is a property of the record: tenant, user, agent, model, tokens split into prompt, completion and cached, cost. Budgets hang off the same rows at three levels, so one runaway agent hits its own cap before it touches the tenant's. Reporting reads the same data back to the tenant, broken down by agent, by user and by model. Think of a context report, the kind that shows what filled a window, applied to money. A tenant can see that one agent produced most of a night's spend. The breakdown names the model it used.

In the run, Tenant A's batch stopped at question 19 of 33 when its budget ran out, one call past the line. The agent's own rate limit stopped its loop on call 7 while the same user's direct call went through. Two different brakes, each attached to the right principal.

Metering plus reporting is what lets a tenant say yes to agents. The cap is the backstop behind them.

5Audit every call

Every request leaves a row that says who made it, on whose behalf, and what it cost. The row is written before the call is forwarded.

Most enterprises cannot see their own agents. A 2026 Cloud Security Alliance survey found 82% had unknown agents in their environments. 65% had an agent incident in the past year [20]. Regulation is moving the same way. The EU AI Act treats event logging as a property of the system itself. Its high-risk deadlines were deferred to 2027 and 2028 rather than dropped [19]. Governance frameworks ask for the same record in plainer language [21], and the measured blind spot in audit and control data keeps growing [22]. When something goes wrong on a shared endpoint, the provider with no record has no answer.

The design is one append-only row per call, written by the gateway before forwarding, joined to the spend record by the call's own id. The row names tenant, mode, subject, actor, model, tokens, cost and status. Rejections are rows too: refused credentials, refused budgets, refused rate limits. The negative space is where incidents live, so it has to be auditable as well.

The run's demo window held 259 calls. Every call joined from audit to spend, including sixteen budget refusals from a setup mistake of ours. The record kept the failure as faithfully as the successes.

Audit is the tenet that turns the other four from claims into evidence.

The proof, told once

A proposal is easy to write. We wanted one working instance, the smallest factory that keeps all five tenets, with a real workload running through it. What follows is that instance, told once, with nothing dressed up.

The test bed

Three machines and one cloud identity provider. The callers ran on a laptop, outside the factory: a runner for Tenant A, a runner for Tenant B, and an agent acting for Tenant B's user. Tenant B's identity provider was ZITADEL Cloud. The gateway ran on a small cloud server: Caddy in front, a 236-line shim for identity and audit, LiteLLM for keys, budgets and the spend ledger, Postgres under both. The model ran on a rented RTX 4090, under vLLM, serving Qwen2.5-Coder-7B-Instruct.

The workload

QASPER, a question-answering dataset built from real research papers, with gold answers scored by token F1. Each paper carries about three questions, so the set was three papers and 33 questions. Both tenants ran the identical set. If the controls changed the work, the scores would show it. Both tenants answered all 33. Mean F1 was 0.456 for Tenant A and 0.457 for Tenant B. The controls did not touch the answers.

The three paths

Tenant A called with a factory key and a budget of $0.0143 for the run. The batch stopped at question 19 of 33, refused by the cap, one call past the line. Tenant B called with tokens from its own identity provider and never held a factory credential. Eleven bad credentials were sent at the gateway during testing. All eleven were refused. Tenant B's agent first did real work, a three-question brief over one paper. Then it looped calls as fast as it could. Its own rate limit stopped it on call 7, at six requests per minute, while a direct call from the same user went through.

The cache test

Both tenants shared one prefix cache on the endpoint. Tenant A warmed the cache on a paper of 4,481 prompt tokens. Tenant B's first call on the same paper then read 0 cached tokens, because the gateway had salted the cache per tenant. With the salting switched off and the cache warmed again, the same call read 4,480 of 4,481. That pair of numbers is the isolation claim, measured both ways.

The audit

The demo window held 259 calls. Every one of them joins from the gateway's audit rows to the gateway's spend log. Two rows are delegation mints and call no model. The rest carry tenant, mode, subject, actor, tokens and cost.

What went sideways

The identity provider did not advertise token exchange, so the gateway validated both tokens and minted the delegation token itself, the fallback the framework allows. During setup for the cache test, Tenant A's own budget cap fired on our repeat passes and stopped them. We raised the cap and reran all five passes. The refused calls stayed in the record.

Cost

The whole run cost about $2.49. The metered price, worked out from the pod's hourly rate and its measured throughput, was about $0.198 per million tokens.

What this does not show

A token factory is a multi-tenant system before it is a model endpoint. Its gateway is the product.

The instance was small: one model, one endpoint, two tenants, one agent. Salting closes the cache channel we measured. The machine underneath is still shared. Delegation ran on the gateway's fallback because the provider did not advertise token exchange. The price came from one pod's measured throughput, with cached tokens billed at the blended rate. A production factory owes more: dedicated endpoints for tenants who need them; reporting a tenant can open without asking.

Five questions to ask any inference provider, including this one. What does a finished task cost on your meter, and how is the price derived? Can my users sign in with our own identity provider? What stops my prompts from showing up in another tenant's timings? When one of my agents runs away, what stops it, and how soon do I see it? After an incident, can you show me the row for the call that caused it?
